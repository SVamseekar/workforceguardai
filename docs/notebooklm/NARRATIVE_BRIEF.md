# Narrative Brief

I built WorkforceGuard AI to sit at the intersection of two things I was already looking at closely: the EU Pay Transparency Directive (2023/970), which forces employers with 100+ workers to publish pay gaps by worker category from 2027 and justify any unadjusted gap over 5%, and a question I didn't think the labour-economics literature had actually answered — does a tight labour market close gender pay gaps on its own, or does the Directive's implicit premise (that disclosure will finish what competition should have already done) not hold up against the data? The commercial and the empirical question turned out to want the same infrastructure: a live, versioned, auditable pipeline over Eurostat data, computing the same composite indices a compliance product would need anyway.

What I built is a two-layer system. The data layer pulls 16 Eurostat datasets — employment, unemployment, job vacancies, gender pay gap by sector and age, labour-market slack and flows, plus contextual series like housing overburden and Gini — across all 27 EU member states, and lands them as Parquet. dbt turns that into a layered warehouse: staging views (one source per model, no joins), core marts (a unified labour-market fact table, four composite indices), and internal marts (a worker-category dimension and internal-vs-market pay benchmarks once a customer uploads real payroll data). DuckDB serves all of this from a single file, read-only, with no database server to operate. On top of that sits a FastAPI backend and a React/TypeScript frontend that present the same numbers as a command-centre dashboard, a comparison tool, a pay-transparency review console, and a governance log.

The four composite indices — Hiring Pressure Index, Labour Resilience, Equity Risk Score, and Transition Readiness — aren't just product dashboard tiles. They're also the object of the accompanying research paper, which uses the same formulas over a 27-country, 2019–2024 Eurostat panel to test whether labour-market tightness and gender pay equity move together. They don't, cleanly: the cross-sectional correlation between employment rate and gender pay gap is weakly *positive* (r ≈ +0.44), which is the opposite sign a naive "tight markets force employers to pay everyone fairly" story would predict, and panel fixed-effects find no significant within-country effect once you control for the country itself. The five tightest labour markets in the sample all sit above the EU27 average pay gap. That's the finding the Directive's disclosure-first theory has to reckon with, and it's why I didn't want the product's numbers and the paper's numbers to be two different codebases — a formula change to improve the dashboard is, deliberately, also a change to the paper's methodology, for better or worse.

Four decisions were load-bearing enough to be worth naming specifically.

First, metrics live in SQL, not in the LLM layer. Every composite index and every pay-transparency classification is computed by a dbt model, driven by a versioned metric registry and stored thresholds — not by an application-code constant, and not by a language model. The "AI copilot" in the product is templated response composition over already-computed numbers, keyword-routed by question category. For a product whose output might end up in front of a works council or a regulator, I wanted every number to be reproducible byte-for-byte from the same warehouse vintage, and I didn't want a hallucination-prone step between raw Eurostat data and a claim about someone's pay gap. That's a real constraint on what the "copilot" can do today, and I'd rather state that plainly than let the label imply more than it delivers.

Second, the audit trail is a SHA-256 hash chain, not a plain log. Every governance action — approve, override, reverse, export — gets an `event_hash` chained to the previous event's hash, verified on read. This detects tampering with the *history* of decisions, which is the actual compliance requirement, as opposed to just recording that decisions happened. It's a narrow guarantee — it doesn't protect against someone who can rewrite the whole chain from genesis, and until authentication was fully wired into every write route, it didn't even guarantee the *actor* field on an event was truthful. I found that gap through an explicit security audit rather than by accident, which is roughly the outcome I'd want from doing the audit at all.

Third, going multi-tenant meant getting DuckDB schema isolation right, and I didn't get it right the first time. Each tenant gets its own DuckDB schema for internal data, layered under a shared analytics warehouse. The first isolation pass looked correct — each tenant reads its own schema — but a follow-up adversarial review found that DuckDB's search-path fallback to the shared `main` schema could still leak a table that existed in `main` but not yet in a given tenant's schema. That's a specific, embarrassing kind of bug: the abstraction you built to prevent leakage has a name-resolution edge case that reintroduces it. I'd rather have found it in review than in a customer's dashboard, and it's the reason there's now a dedicated tenant-isolation test suite instead of trusting the schema design by inspection.

Fourth, worker category — not company-wide average — is the unit the whole internal-data model is built around, because that's the actual unit the Directive asks employers to report and justify against. It's a harder data-ingestion problem for a customer (you need a job architecture that can plausibly represent "equal value" groupings, not just a flat payroll export), which is part of why the shipped internal data is still sample/synthetic rather than a real customer's.

Current status, plainly: the public data pipeline, the composite indices, the multi-tenant auth, the hash-chained governance log, and the CI/deploy gates are real and running in production. What's early or gated: Transition Readiness is explicitly labeled in-development in the metric registry; internal company data is synthetic pending real customer onboarding; evidence-pack export is raw JSON without cryptographic signing or PDF rendering, as of the last full internal review I have visibility into. The paper is a working paper (MPRA), not peer-reviewed, and I'm careful to say so.

The open question I'm still sitting with is less about engineering and more about the empirical result itself: if tight labour markets don't close gender pay gaps, and disclosure alone hasn't been shown to either, what's actually doing the work in the cases where gaps *do* narrow? My working hypothesis, following some adjacent literature on German pay-transparency law working only where works councils exist, is that it's institutional bargaining power — not market tightness, not disclosure by itself — that's the missing conditioning variable. I haven't built that analysis yet. If it holds, it changes the product's own framing too: a compliance tool that just publishes numbers might be necessary but not sufficient, and the more useful long-run feature might be surfacing *where* an employer's own governance structures (works councils, union density, collective bargaining coverage) put them in a position to act on what the numbers show.
